# Dissertation Abstract

This dissertation investigates whether a reservoir-computing controller can inherit useful locomotion structure from multiple friction-specific central pattern generator (CPG) teachers and use that structure to adapt an Ant robot across different floor surfaces. The study uses three surface conditions, friction 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5, and trains a pipeline in which each surface is first modeled by a separate CPG teacher, then used to generate supervised rollout data for an autoencoder and Echo State Network readout, followed by closed-loop DAgger-style fine-tuning.

The main contribution is not simply the reservoir model itself, but the combination of friction-conditioned teachers, a compact latent representation, and a tuned quadruped gait design. Earlier versions produced unstable behavior such as diagonal-leg hanging and one-leg compensation. The gait teacher was revised into a stance-heavy quadruped walk with friction-specific phase spacing and duty-factor control, producing motion that is more readable and more consistent with animal-like four-legged locomotion.

The final saved model shows surface-dependent performance rather than uniform success across all frictions. The latest summary reports RC forward distances of -0.108 on friction 0.5, 1.125 on friction 1.0, and 2.672 on friction 1.5, with corresponding CPG distances of 0.147, 3.565, and 6.762. These results support a conservative conclusion: the architecture is capable of surface-conditioned adaptation and improved gait structure, but the final closed-loop policy does not yet match the best teacher behavior equally well on every surface.

The dissertation implication is that reservoir computing is a viable learned adaptation layer when paired with multiple surface-specific CPG priors, but performance remains sensitive to teacher design and training schedule. The strongest evidence for this claim is the combination of the tuned model summary and the visual rollout clips that show clearer motion distinctions across surfaces.
